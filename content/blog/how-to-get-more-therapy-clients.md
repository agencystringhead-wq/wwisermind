If your caseload has thinned out this year, you're in good company. In the [2026 Clinician Well-Being Study](https://www.simplepractice.com/resource/2026-clinician-well-being-study/) from SimplePractice and Heard, clinicians named marketing and client acquisition as the most stressful part of running a practice, ahead of taxes and documentation.

The good news is that getting more clients rarely means doing more marketing. It usually means fixing a few quiet leaks, then showing up in the places people now look. Here are nine things that work, in the order we'd do them.

## 1. Fix the leaks before you chase traffic

Most therapy websites don't have a traffic problem. They have a booking problem. People land, look around, feel unsure, and leave.

Open your website on your phone and pretend you're someone looking for help late at night. Can you tell, within five seconds, who this therapist helps, where they practice, and how to book? If not, start there. More visitors won't help if the ones you already have are leaving.

A quick test: ask a friend who isn't a therapist to find your fee, your availability and how to book. Time it. If it takes more than a minute, a nervous client probably won't bother.

## 2. Say exactly who you help, in the first line

"Welcome to my practice" tells a visitor nothing. "I help adults in Denver with panic attacks and health anxiety, in person and by telehealth across Colorado" tells them they're in the right place.

That one sentence does a lot of work. It belongs at the top of your homepage, in your Google Business Profile, in every directory listing, and in your social bios. It's also exactly the kind of clear statement Google and AI tools look for when deciding who to recommend.

## 3. Give each specialty its own page

When someone searches "EMDR therapist in Austin" or "couples counseling Chicago," Google wants to show a page about that exact thing. A single "Services" page with eight bullet points can't compete with a practice that has a full page on each.

A good specialty page covers who it's for, what the struggle feels like from the inside, how you work with it, what sessions look like, and what tends to change. Write it for the person, not for a fellow clinician.

## 4. Put your fees on your website

This one makes a lot of therapists uncomfortable, but hiding your fee rarely helps. People assume the worst, or move on to someone who's upfront.

A calm fees page includes your session fee and length, sliding scale details if you offer one, which insurance you take (or a clear "I'm out-of-network"), how superbills work, and your cancellation policy. If you're private pay, explain out-of-network benefits in two or three sentences. Many clients don't know their plan may reimburse part of the cost.

## 5. Make booking the easy part

Reaching out to a therapist takes courage. If someone finally works up the nerve and hits voicemail, a broken form or a week of silence, many simply give up.

Give people more than one way in: a self-booking link for a free consult call, a short contact form, and a phone number with a voicemail greeting that says when you'll call back. Set an auto-reply on your form and email with your response time and crisis resources. And aim to reply within one business day.

## 6. Claim your Google Business Profile (and the other two)

When people search "therapist near me," Google's map results usually show up before anything else. Your Google Business Profile is how you get there.

Pick the right primary category, write a description with your specialties, upload real photos, and keep your hours accurate. If you're telehealth only, you can hide your street address. Then set up **Bing Places** (which feeds ChatGPT and Copilot) and **Apple Business Connect** (Apple Maps and Siri). It takes an afternoon, and most practices skip at least one.

## 7. Get ready for AI search

More clients now ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Google's AI answers for a therapist before they ever visit a directory. Those tools build their answers from the public web, so the job is to make sure what the web says about you is clear and consistent.

Try it yourself. In a private browser window, ask ChatGPT: "What do you know about [your name], [your license], in [your city]?" If it draws a blank or gets details wrong, fix your website and listings first. You don't need llms.txt files or special "AI hacks." Google itself says that optimizing for AI search is still SEO. ## 8. Build referrals that don't depend on an algorithm

Directories and search results change. Relationships don't, at least not as fast. A handful of steady referral sources can carry a practice through a slow season.

Think about who sees your ideal clients before you do: primary care doctors, pediatricians, OB-GYNs, school counselors, psychiatrists, divorce attorneys, other therapists with full caseloads or different specialties. Reach out with something useful, like a one-page summary of who you help and how to refer. Then follow up, and refer back when you can. When you can't take someone, a warm referral to a colleague is good care, and it's remembered.

## 9. Stay ethical with reviews

In most industries, the advice is "ask every customer for a review." In yours, the ACA, APA, NASW and AAMFT codes all restrict soliciting testimonials, and HIPAA limits how you use client information in marketing.

You can claim your Google profile and let reviews come in on their own. You can put a plain link to it on your website. What you shouldn't do is ask current clients for reviews, offer incentives, or reply in a way that confirms someone is your client. Build trust with a warm About page, real credentials, and clear policies instead.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why am I not getting therapy clients?

The most common reasons are that people can't find you, or they find you and leave without reaching out. Check whether your website says clearly who you help, lists your fees, and makes booking easy. Then check whether your Google Business Profile and directory listings are complete and consistent.

### How long does it take to fill a therapy caseload?

It varies a lot by location, specialty, insurance mix and how visible you are online. Website and booking fixes can help within weeks, because they affect visitors you already get. SEO and referral relationships usually take a few months to build.

### Is Psychology Today still worth it?

For some therapists, yes. Many report fewer referrals than a few years ago, while Psychology Today says overall traffic is steady. Track how many clients it actually sends you, and don't let it be your only source of new clients.

### Can therapists ask clients for Google reviews?

Generally no. Most professional ethics codes restrict soliciting testimonials, especially from current clients. You can have a Google profile clients can review on their own, but check your licensing board's exact rules before doing anything more.

### Should I take insurance to get more clients?

Insurance can bring in more inquiries, but often at lower rates and with more paperwork. Many therapists now mix the two, or go private pay and offer superbills for out-of-network reimbursement. There's no single right answer; it depends on your market and your finances.
