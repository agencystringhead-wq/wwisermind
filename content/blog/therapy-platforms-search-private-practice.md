## What therapists are seeing

In early October, WHYY ran a long piece on Philadelphia-area therapists and what one of them called the "Uberization" of counseling. It's worth reading in full.

One trauma therapist with eight years in private practice described referrals falling 10% in 2023, another 20% in 2024, and another 25% after that. Her Psychology Today listing and Google searches, once steady, slowed to almost nothing. She blamed platforms that pour money into search and crowd out independent practices. She'd even found herself listed on platform sites she never joined, apparently as bait to redirect people searching for her by name.

And it's not one story. SimplePractice's 2026 clinician survey found marketing and client acquisition was the most stressful part of running a practice, ahead of taxes and documentation.

So the worry is real. But the picture isn't hopeless, and I'd push back on the "private practice is dying" mood a little.

## Why you can still win

Platforms are built to win broad, national searches. "Online therapy." "Affordable therapist." "Therapist near me" in a hundred cities at once. They're very good at that.

They're much weaker at the specific, local, personal searches that actually fill a caseload. Things like "EMDR therapist Media PA," "couples counselor who sees LGBTQ clients in Ballard," or just your name. A platform can't put a real office on Google Maps under your address. It can't write about your approach the way you would. And people choosing a therapist, more than almost any other service, want to know who they'll be sitting with.

That's your ground. Here's how to hold it.

## 1. Search your own name

Start here, because it's free and it takes five minutes. Search your name, your name plus "therapist," and your practice name, in a private browser window.

If a platform shows you as available when you've never joined, ask them in writing to remove the listing. Keep a record. Then make sure your own site, Google profile and directory listings show up first, with the same name, address and phone everywhere.

## 2. Treat your Google Business Profile like a second homepage

For local searches, the map results often sit above everything else. That's prime space, and platforms can't take it from you.

Fill in every field. Pick accurate categories, list your services, add real photos of your office (not stock), and keep hours current, including telehealth-only hours if that's how you work. Our free [Google Business Profile checklist](/freebies/google-business-profile-checklist-for-therapists/) walks through it.

## 3. Build pages for what you actually treat

A single "Services" page listing ten issues won't rank for any of them. One focused page per specialty usually does better: who it's for, what sessions look like, your training in it, fees, and how to start.

Add your town or neighborhood where it's honest to do so. Don't spin up thin pages for twenty suburbs you don't serve. Google spots that, and so do readers.

## 4. Show the person, not just the practice

This is the part platforms flatten. Their profiles look alike by design.

Your site can do better. A real photo. A bio that sounds like you talk. Your fees, stated plainly. What the first session involves. Your license number and the states you practice in. We go deeper on this in [YMYL and E-E-A-T for Therapy Websites](/blog/ymyl-eeat-therapy-website/), but the short version is that trust signals help both Google and the anxious person reading at midnight.

## 5. Build a referral network that doesn't depend on search

The same WHYY therapist got her two newest clients from people she'd seen before. Word of mouth still works.

So do professional referrals. Family doctors, school counselors, psychiatrists, and other therapists with full caseloads all send people somewhere. A short, specific note about who you help (and who you don't) makes you easy to refer to. Just follow your profession's ethics code on testimonials and soliciting current clients.

## 6. Don't ignore AI search

More people now ask ChatGPT or Google's AI answers to recommend a therapist. Those tools pull from the same signals: clear specialty pages, consistent listings, a complete Google profile. ## What about staying on a platform?

No shame in it. Plenty of clinicians in that WHYY piece use Headway or Grow for insurance billing and are fine with the trade. The risk is relying on one for every new client, because you don't control the price or the rules.

A sensible middle path: keep the platform for what it does well, and quietly build a front door that's yours. Then, if the terms change, you have somewhere to go.

## Quick checklist

- Searched your name and practice name in a private window
- Asked any platform listing you without permission to remove it
- Google Business Profile complete, with real photos
- One page per specialty, with fees and how to start
- Bio, license and states listed clearly
- A short note sent to five local referrers
